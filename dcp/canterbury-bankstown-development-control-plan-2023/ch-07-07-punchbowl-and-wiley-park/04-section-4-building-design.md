### 4.1 Minimum lot sizes

A minimum lot size for certain residential dwelling types is required to ensure lots are large enough to accommodate the development. This particularly applies to residential flat buildings to ensure that adequate frontage and amenity are provided.

#### Objectives

O1. Ensure lots are large enough to accommodate required setbacks and separation to adjoining properties, including ground floor outdoor uses such as private open space and landscaped areas, driveways, play areas, pedestrian access, set down and pick up areas, carparks and vehicle manoeuvring areas.

O2. Minimise adverse impacts of development on the amenity of the area.

O3. Require lot amalgamation to achieve minimum lot frontage and size requirements.

O4. Ensure lots are large enough to protect special attributes, including natural or cultural features, heritage items, heritage conservation areas, trees and natural topographical features.

O5. Prevent fragmentation or isolation of land.

### 4.2	 The building envelope

#### 4.2.1 Building heights and storeys

The maximum building heights in the Precinct are stipulated in the CBLEP 2023. This section of the DCP provides further guidance on the bulk, scale and design of buildings, ensuring for careful consideration of impacts on streets, shared lanes, open space and pedestrian links.

#### Objectives

O1. To design taller buildings that allow for adequate provision of sunlight to streets and open space for pedestrian amenity and to allow trees to grow.

O2. To design a distinctive and legible skyline to identify the Village Centres and Metro stations.

O3. To ensure buildings are designed in consideration of wind impacts to the public domain.

O4. To design elegant slender towers that have consideration for view sharing.

O5. To ensure taller buildings express a strong architectural response defined by a clearly identified concept or idea to create a sense of place.

O6. To provide for landmark towers on prominent corners to improve the legibility of the Precinct.

#### 4.2.3 Street wall

Together with the public domain, the street wall with active street frontage is the built element that shapes the way most of the city is experienced. The street wall provides spatial enclosure and must operate in concert with other street walls or podiums to form a satisfying and rich experience of the public spaces of the city or place for the community. Interruptions or erosions of the street wall generally work to undermine the vitality and definition of the street wall and are not favoured.

#### Objectives

O1. To ensure that the visual relationship between the street and public space is reinforced.

O2. To design the street wall to achieve fine grain modulation to create visual interest and variety while ensuring for consistency in streetscape character.

O3. To create a vertical rhythm and visual interest to reduce the scale of building mass.

O4. To implement an articulation zone within podiums and facades to allow architectural form to develop.

#### 4.2.5 Upper storey setbacks Objectives

O1. To ensure buildings are setback above street walls to reinforce the scale of streets, mitigate wind down drafts to pedestrians and dominance of the public domain.

O2. To ensure that upper storey setbacks allow for the enjoyment of sun and daylight within public spaces, provide for views of the sky and allow trees to grow.

O3. To provide for appropriate transition and sympathetic response to lower-scaled buildings and significant elements within the local context.

O4. To create opportunities for tree canopy, planting and outdoor open space on podiums.

#### Principles

P1. Clearly delineate the street wall by using upper storey setbacks and create a visual break in tall building facades.

P2. Encourage green cover and useable spaces on podiums and rooftops.

P3. Promote generous commercial, communal and residential balconies on upper storey setbacks.

P4. The taller portion of the building is setback above a podium to ensure the bulk and scale is sympathetic to existing and future development and to streets. This mitigates dominance to pedestrians at street level.

#### 4.2.6 Building separation Objectives

O1. To ensure sunlight penetrates through to street level to protect the amenity of pedestrians and allow street trees to grow.

O2. To ensure that taller buildings are not overwhelming and overcrowded and appear as distinct elements within the skyline with space around them.

O3. To provide adequate privacy, access to light, air and outlook to the sky for the occupants of buildings, neighbouring properties and future buildings.

O4. To ensure development does not unfairly limit the re-development potential of adjoining sites in the future.

#### Principles

P1. Building design should avoid using multiple upper-level setback ‘steps’ that result in a ‘wedding cake’ or a ‘ziggurat’ appearance.

P2. Maintain adequate separation distances between buildings on the same site to protect access to sunlight and sky views and improve air circulation between buildings.

P3. Provide separation to enable a building to be seen from all sides and to allow for articulation that responds to orientation.

#### 4.3.2 Active street frontages and edges

Active street frontages are continuous  retail and business uses with frequent openings that engage with the street and public space, creating a lively, welcoming environment that encourages pedestrian activity.  To create a human and scale,' active street frontages should feature a fine grain of narrow shopfronts with services and utilities positioned away from the primary frontage. Active edges are incidental active uses along a frontage. While secondary to active street frontages, they contribute positively to the public domain. For example, Canterbury Road would primarily includes business uses that will have less foot traffic but still provide occasional active use to service workers and pedestrians along this street. In residential areas, an active edge could include a café or corner shop, on a lane, pedestrian link or open space, with the remainder of the ground floor frontage being garden apartments with individual entries. Active uses typically include retail, customer service areas, cafes, restaurants and outdoor dining areas. To enhance the pedestrian comfort and encourage street life, active street frontages must incorporate continuous awnings. Awnings should also be provided along active edges, where possible, although they do not need to extend along the entire length of the frontage.

Image - Active frontages along sharedway at Marrickville Metro, Marrickville, NSW (Source: Architectus)

> [Picture on page 84, not reproduced as text; see the original PDF.]

#### Objectives

O1. To encourage finer grained narrow frontages with a high standard of design to create varied experiences that provide views into and out of shops.

O2. To ensure that Punchbowl Road, The Boulevarde and Lakemba Street have a high quality and engaging public realm.

O3. To ensure that the interface and amenity of Canterbury Road is improved for pedestrians.

O4. To ensure frontages along key streets, pedestrian links, lanes and open spaces provide both active and passive surveillance.

O5. To encourage people to linger and enjoy public space.

O6. To ensure that building services do not dominate the pedestrian experience and are designed as an integrated part of the building form.

O7. To create opportunities to enjoy the positive aspects of climate and shield people from the negative such as wind, rain and heat.

O8. To provide secondary active edges that create vitality and interest on frontages with less foot traffic.
